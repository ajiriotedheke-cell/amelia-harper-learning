A Simple 20-Minute Calming Bedtime Routine for Toddlers and Kids (Ages 2–7)

Try a gentle 20-minute bedtime routine for young children, with tidy-up time, brushing teeth, a screen-free story and a reassuring goodnight.

Amelia Harper Kids · Practical family guide

Bedtime can be a busy part of the day. Children may want one more game, one more drink or one more story, while adults are trying to bring the household to a close. A short, familiar sequence can make the transition easier because children know what comes next. This 20-minute plan is a flexible example for families with children roughly ages 2–7. Adjust the timing to suit your child's needs and your household routine.

The 20-minute routine at a glance

Think of the routine as three simple parts: prepare for sleep, enjoy a quiet story, and finish with connection. The exact minutes are a guide rather than a rule. If your family needs longer, spread the steps out; if a child is already tired, keep the routine shorter and calm.

Step 1: Tidy up and brush teeth (5 minutes)

Give a gentle warning before play ends: “In five minutes, we will put the toys away and get ready for bed.” Turn tidying into a small shared task. You might ask your child to put soft toys in one basket while you put books on a shelf. Avoid making it a race or attaching a reward that creates pressure.

Move to the bathroom and follow the same hygiene sequence each evening: brush teeth with age-appropriate supervision, wash hands or face as needed, and change into comfortable sleepwear. A simple picture checklist can help a child remember the order. Adults should continue to provide the level of help and supervision appropriate to the child's age.

Amelia Harper Kids' Good Manners Songs page introduces themes such as healthy daily habits, but the listed themes are planned collection topics rather than a promise that a specific brushing-teeth song is currently available. You can also make up a short tidy-up tune together.

Step 2: Enjoy a screen-free audio story or book (8–10 minutes)

Once the practical tasks are finished, choose one quiet activity. Read a printed picture book, tell a familiar story, or listen to an audio story with an adult nearby. If you choose audio, prepare the device before the child settles, keep the screen out of the main activity where possible, and avoid autoplaying unrelated videos or recommendations.

Families can listen to Tumi and the Little Star on the Amelia Harper Kids stories page. The page provides an audio player and a short description; it does not currently provide a written transcript. Sit together if your child wants company, or stay close enough to respond if they have a question. Audio is one option, not a sleep treatment, and it may not suit every child.

For a screen-free alternative, put the device away and read a paper book. Let your child look at the pictures, point to a favourite character or simply listen. The aim is a calm shared moment rather than finishing a set number of pages.

Step 3: Gentle reflection and goodnight (5 minutes)

Finish with a predictable phrase, a cuddle if welcomed, a short prayer or song if that is part of your family life, and a goodnight. You can invite a little conversation without turning the moment into a quiz. Try one or two of these prompts:

  • What was your favourite part of Tumi's adventure?
  • What did you imagine when you heard about the little star?
  • Was there a kind or helpful thing someone did in the story?
  • What is one thing you enjoyed about today?

Some children will answer at length; others may prefer to listen or say “I don't know.” Accept either response. A quiet goodnight can be enough.

What if your child resists the routine?

Start with fewer steps and repeat them in the same order. Offer two manageable choices, such as choosing between two books or two pairs of pyjamas, rather than reopening the whole bedtime plan. Give transition warnings and keep your language brief and reassuring. If the routine is consistently difficult, consider whether the timing, environment or sequence needs to change. Persistent sleep concerns are best discussed with a qualified healthcare professional.

Make the routine work for your family

A bedtime routine should support connection, not become another source of pressure. Some evenings will be disrupted by travel, illness, visitors or ordinary family life. Return to the familiar sequence when you can. Explore more ideas in our family screen-time guide and browse learning resources for activities that can be enjoyed away from a screen.

Prepare the environment before the routine begins

A little preparation can make the 20 minutes feel less rushed. Put the storybook or audio choice where an adult can reach it, make sure pyjamas are ready and reduce unnecessary trips between rooms. If siblings share a bedtime space, agree on a quiet activity they can do together. Keep the routine predictable, but do not worry if one evening takes longer than another.

For younger children, a simple picture sequence can show the three stages: bathroom, story, goodnight. Point to the next picture rather than repeating many instructions. Older children may prefer to help choose the book or prepare the room. Keep choices limited and offer them before the child becomes overtired.

Make the story a connection, not another task

When listening to Tumi and the Little Star, there is no need to test whether your child remembers every detail. You can pause briefly if they want to comment, or let the story play without interruption. Some children enjoy talking afterwards; others settle more easily with a short goodnight and no further questions. Follow the child's cues and avoid extending the routine with a long discussion if they are ready to rest.

If audio does not suit your household, the same quiet moment can come from a printed book, a made-up story or a familiar lullaby. Keep the device out of reach where possible, switch off unrelated notifications and do not let autoplay move into videos or other content. The adult should choose and start the audio, then remain available to supervise.

Most importantly, use the routine as a reassuring pattern rather than a strict performance. Consistency can help children know what to expect, while flexibility helps the routine fit real family life.

A gentle reminder: These are general activity ideas, not individual educational, developmental or medical assessments. Adapt activities to your child's interests and abilities, supervise online and hands-on play, and take breaks when needed.
